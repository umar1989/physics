Sound

How sound is produced and travels, characteristics of a sound wave, speed of sound, echo and reverberation, range of hearing, ultrasound and SONAR.

Production of sound

Sound is a form of energy that produces the sensation of hearing. It is produced by vibrating objects. A vibrating tuning fork makes a ping-pong ball touching it bounce; a struck bell or a plucked guitar string vibrates; our voice is produced by the vibration of the vocal cords.

Propagation of sound

  • Sound needs a material medium (solid, liquid or gas) to travel. It cannot travel through vacuum. The bell-jar experiment shows this: as air is pumped out of the jar, the sound of the ringing bell becomes fainter.
  • The particles of the medium do not travel from the source to the ear. Each particle vibrates about its mean position and passes the disturbance on to the next particle.
  • In air, sound travels as a longitudinal wave: the particles vibrate parallel to the direction in which the wave travels.
Compression and rarefaction

A compression (C) is a region where particles are crowded together: high pressure and density. A rarefaction (R) is a region where particles are spread apart: low pressure and density. A sound wave is a series of compressions and rarefactions moving through the medium.

In a transverse wave (such as a wave on a stretched string or ripples on water), particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of the wave. Sound in air is not transverse.

Pitch, loudness and quality

  • Pitch depends on frequency. Higher frequency → higher (shriller) pitch. A woman’s voice usually has a higher pitch than a man’s; a bird’s chirp is high-pitched, a lion’s roar is low-pitched.
  • Loudness depends on amplitude. Larger amplitude → louder sound, because it carries more energy.
  • Quality (timbre) lets us tell apart two sounds of the same pitch and loudness, such as a violin and a flute playing the same note.
  • A sound of a single frequency is a tone; a mixture of frequencies is a note. An unpleasant sound is called noise.

Speed of sound in different media

  • Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases: \( v_{\text{solid}} > v_{\text{liquid}} > v_{\text{gas}} \).
  • The speed of sound increases with the temperature of the medium.
  • Speed of sound in air: about 331 m/s at 0 °C and 346 m/s at 25 °C. In water it is about 1500 m/s, and in steel about 5960 m/s.
  • Light is much faster than sound, which is why we see lightning before we hear thunder.

Reflection of sound and echo

Sound reflects from a solid or liquid surface, just like light, obeying the same laws of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and the incident sound, the reflected sound and the normal lie in the same plane.

Echo

An echo is the sound heard again after reflection from a distant obstacle such as a cliff or a large building.

The sensation of a sound lasts in our brain for about 0.1 s. To hear a distinct echo, the reflected sound must reach us at least 0.1 s after the original. In 0.1 s, sound in air (at 344 m/s, about 22 °C) travels 34.4 m, which is the distance to the obstacle and back. So the obstacle must be at least 17.2 m away.

Distance of reflecting surface
\( d = \dfrac{v \times t}{2} \)
t = time between the sound and its echo

Reverberation

The persistence of sound in a big hall because of repeated reflections from walls, ceiling and floor is called reverberation. Too much reverberation makes speech unclear. It is reduced by covering the walls and ceiling with sound-absorbing materials such as fibreboard, rough plaster and curtains, and by using padded seats.

Uses of multiple reflection of sound

  • Megaphones, loudspeakers, horns and shehnais have a cone shape that reflects sound forwards towards the audience.
  • Stethoscope: the patient’s heartbeat reaches the doctor’s ears by multiple reflections inside the tube.
  • Curved ceilings of concert halls and a soundboard behind the stage spread sound evenly across the hall.

Range of hearing

  • Humans can hear sounds of frequency from about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz (20 kHz). This range narrows with age.
  • Sounds below 20 Hz are infrasonic (infrasound). Elephants, whales and rhinoceroses produce them; earthquakes produce infrasound before the main shock waves.
  • Sounds above 20 kHz are ultrasonic (ultrasound). Dolphins, bats and porpoises produce them; dogs can hear them.

Applications of ultrasound

  • Cleaning hard-to-reach parts such as spiral tubes and electronic components.
  • Detecting cracks and flaws inside metal blocks.
  • Echocardiography (images of the heart) and ultrasonography (images of internal organs and of a foetus).
  • Breaking small kidney stones into fine grains.

SONAR

SONAR (Sound Navigation And Ranging) uses ultrasonic waves to measure the depth of the sea and to locate underwater objects such as submarines, sunken ships and icebergs. A transmitter sends ultrasound; it reflects from the object and a detector receives it. If the echo returns after time \(t\) and the speed of sound in seawater is \(v\), then the distance is \( d = vt/2 \). Bats use the same idea (echolocation) to fly and find food in the dark.

Structure of the human ear

The outer ear (pinna) collects sound and directs it through the ear canal to the eardrum (tympanic membrane), which vibrates. In the middle ear, three small bones (hammer, anvil and stirrup) amplify these vibrations and pass them to the inner ear. There the cochlea converts them into electrical signals, which travel along the auditory nerve to the brain.

Solved examples

A sound wave has a frequency of 2 kHz and a wavelength of 35 cm. How long will it take to travel 1.5 km?

\( v = \nu\lambda = 2000 \times 0.35 = 700\ \text{m/s} \)

\( t = \dfrac{d}{v} = \dfrac{1500}{700} = 2.14\ \text{s} \)

Time ≈ 2.1 s.

A tuning fork vibrates at 512 Hz. Find its time period, and the wavelength of the sound it produces in air where the speed of sound is 344 m/s.

\( T = \dfrac{1}{\nu} = \dfrac{1}{512} = 0.00195\ \text{s} \approx 1.95 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s} \)

\( \lambda = \dfrac{v}{\nu} = \dfrac{344}{512} = 0.67\ \text{m} \)

T ≈ 1.95 ms; λ ≈ 0.67 m.

A person claps near a cliff and hears the echo after 2 s. If the speed of sound is 346 m/s, how far away is the cliff?

The sound goes to the cliff and comes back in 2 s.

\( d = \dfrac{vt}{2} = \dfrac{346 \times 2}{2} = 346\ \text{m} \)

The cliff is 346 m away.

A ship sends ultrasound that returns from the seabed after 3.42 s. If the speed of ultrasound in seawater is 1531 m/s, find the depth of the sea.

\( d = \dfrac{vt}{2} = \dfrac{1531 \times 3.42}{2} = 2618\ \text{m} \)

Depth ≈ 2618 m ≈ 2.62 km.

Show that the minimum distance for hearing a distinct echo in air is 17.2 m. (Speed of sound = 344 m/s; persistence of hearing = 0.1 s.)

Total distance travelled in 0.1 s \( = 344 \times 0.1 = 34.4\ \text{m} \). This is to the obstacle and back.

Minimum distance \( = 34.4 / 2 = 17.2\ \text{m} \).

Quick revision

  • Sound is produced by vibrations and needs a medium; it cannot travel through vacuum.
  • Sound in air is a longitudinal wave of compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure).
  • \( \nu = 1/T \) and \( v = \nu\lambda \). Frequency is in hertz (Hz).
  • Pitch ↔ frequency; loudness ↔ amplitude; quality (timbre) distinguishes instruments.
  • Speed: solids > liquids > gases; increases with temperature. About 346 m/s in air at 25 °C.
  • Echo needs a delay of at least 0.1 s → obstacle at least 17.2 m away. Distance \( d = vt/2 \).
  • Reverberation is reduced with sound-absorbing materials.
  • Human hearing: 20 Hz – 20 kHz. Below: infrasound. Above: ultrasound (used in SONAR, medical imaging, cleaning, crack detection).
