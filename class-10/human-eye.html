The Human Eye and the Colourful World

Structure of the eye, power of accommodation, defects of vision and their correction, refraction and dispersion through a prism, atmospheric refraction and scattering of light.

The human eye

The eye works like a camera. Its lens forms a real, inverted image of an object on a light-sensitive screen called the retina. The brain interprets the signals so that we see the object upright.

PartFunction
CorneaThin transparent membrane at the front. Most of the refraction of light entering the eye happens here.
IrisDark muscular diaphragm behind the cornea that controls the size of the pupil. It gives the eye its colour.
PupilOpening in the iris that controls how much light enters. It becomes smaller in bright light and larger in dim light.
Eye lensTransparent, flexible convex lens made of jelly-like material. It fine-tunes the focal length to focus objects at different distances.
Ciliary musclesHold the lens and change its curvature, and therefore its focal length.
RetinaLight-sensitive layer at the back with rod and cone cells. It converts light into electrical signals.
Optic nerveCarries the electrical signals from the retina to the brain.

Power of accommodation

Definition

The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length so that both near and distant objects are focused on the retina is called the power of accommodation.

  • To see distant objects, the ciliary muscles relax, the lens becomes thinner, and its focal length increases.
  • To see nearby objects, the ciliary muscles contract, the lens becomes thicker, and its focal length decreases.
  • Near point (least distance of distinct vision) of a normal young adult eye is about 25 cm.
  • Far point of a normal eye is at infinity.
  • A normal eye can therefore see clearly anything between 25 cm and infinity.

Cataract: in old age the eye lens can become milky and cloudy, causing partial or complete loss of vision. It can be treated by surgery.

Refraction of light through a prism

A glass prism has two triangular bases and three rectangular faces. The angle between two of its refracting faces is called the angle of the prism. When a ray passes through a prism, it bends towards the base at each face. The angle between the incident ray (extended) and the emergent ray is the angle of deviation.

Unlike a glass slab, the two refracting faces of a prism are not parallel, so the emergent ray is not parallel to the incident ray.

Dispersion of white light

Definition

The splitting of white light into its component colours when it passes through a prism is called dispersion. The band of colours is called a spectrum.

  • The order of colours is VIBGYOR: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red.
  • Different colours bend by different amounts. Red bends the least; violet bends the most.
  • Isaac Newton showed that a second, inverted prism recombines the spectrum into white light. So white light is made up of seven colours.

Rainbow: a natural spectrum seen in the sky after a shower, always opposite to the Sun. Tiny water droplets act like small prisms: they refract and disperse sunlight, reflect it internally, and refract it again as it comes out.

Atmospheric refraction

The air in the atmosphere has layers of different temperatures and densities, so its refractive index changes from layer to layer. Light passing through it is refracted many times. This is atmospheric refraction.

  • Twinkling of stars: starlight bends continuously as it passes through changing air layers. Stars are so far away that they act as point sources, so the apparent position and brightness of a star keep changing, and it twinkles.
  • Planets do not twinkle: they are much closer and act as extended sources (collections of many points). The changes from different points average out.
  • Stars appear higher than they are, because the light bends towards the normal as it enters denser layers of air.
  • Advance sunrise and delayed sunset: we see the Sun about 2 minutes before it actually rises above the horizon and about 2 minutes after it actually sets. This makes the day about 4 minutes longer.
  • Objects seen through hot air above a fire appear to flicker.

Scattering of light

When light strikes very small particles (dust, smoke, air molecules), part of it is sent off in different directions. This is scattering. How much a colour is scattered depends on the size of the particles and on the wavelength of the light.

  • Tyndall effect: the path of a beam of light becomes visible when it passes through a colloid, such as sunlight entering a dusty room through a small hole, or sunlight passing through the canopy of a dense forest.
  • Very fine particles (air molecules) scatter shorter wavelengths (blue) much more than longer wavelengths (red). Larger particles scatter all colours about equally, so the scattered light looks white (as in clouds).
  • Blue sky: air molecules scatter blue light from sunlight far more than red, and this scattered blue light reaches our eyes from all directions.
  • Sky looks dark to astronauts at very high altitudes, because there is almost no air there to scatter light.
  • Reddish Sun at sunrise and sunset: sunlight travels a much longer path through the atmosphere near the horizon. Most of the blue light is scattered away, and mainly red light reaches us.
  • Danger signals are red: red light is scattered the least by fog and smoke, so it can be seen from the farthest distance.

Solved examples

The far point of a myopic person is 80 cm in front of the eye. What is the nature and power of the lens needed to correct the problem?

The lens must make an object at infinity form a virtual image at the far point: \( u = -\infty,\ v = -80\ \text{cm} \).

\( \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{v} - \dfrac{1}{u} = -\dfrac{1}{80} - 0 \Rightarrow f = -80\ \text{cm} = -0.8\ \text{m} \)

\( P = \dfrac{1}{-0.8} = -1.25\ \text{D} \)

A concave lens of power −1.25 D.

The near point of a hypermetropic eye is 1 m. What lens is needed so that the person can read at the normal near point of 25 cm?

The lens must form a virtual image at 1 m of a book held at 25 cm: \( u = -25\ \text{cm},\ v = -100\ \text{cm} \).

\( \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{v} - \dfrac{1}{u} = -\dfrac{1}{100} + \dfrac{1}{25} = \dfrac{-1 + 4}{100} = \dfrac{3}{100} \Rightarrow f = 33.3\ \text{cm} \)

\( P = \dfrac{100}{33.3} = +3.0\ \text{D} \)

A convex lens of power +3.0 D.

A person needs a lens of power −5.5 D for distant vision and +1.5 D for near vision. Find the focal lengths of these lenses.

Distant vision: \( f = \dfrac{1}{P} = \dfrac{1}{-5.5}\ \text{m} = -0.182\ \text{m} = -18.2\ \text{cm} \) (concave lens).

Near vision: \( f = \dfrac{1}{1.5}\ \text{m} = 0.667\ \text{m} = +66.7\ \text{cm} \) (convex lens).

−18.2 cm and +66.7 cm.

Quick revision

  • Eye lens forms a real, inverted image on the retina. Most refraction happens at the cornea; the pupil controls the light entering.
  • Power of accommodation: ciliary muscles change the lens’s focal length. Near point 25 cm; far point infinity.
  • Myopia: image in front of retina → concave lens. Hypermetropia: image behind retina → convex lens. Presbyopia: old age → convex or bifocal lens.
  • Prism: light bends towards the base; angle of deviation. Emergent ray not parallel to incident ray.
  • Dispersion: VIBGYOR. Red deviates least, violet most.
  • Atmospheric refraction: twinkling of stars, planets don’t twinkle, advance sunrise and delayed sunset (about 2 min each).
  • Scattering: Tyndall effect; blue sky; red sunrise and sunset; red danger signals; dark sky in space.
