Electricity

Electric current and potential difference, Ohm’s law, resistance and resistivity, resistors in series and parallel, heating effect of current and electric power.

Electric charge and current

Electric charge is carried by electrons (negative) and protons (positive). The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C). The charge on one electron is \( e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C} \), so 1 C is the charge of about \( 6 \times 10^{18} \) electrons.

Electric current

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor.

Current
\( I = \dfrac{Q}{t} \)
SI unit: ampere (A) = C/s
Charge from electrons
\( Q = ne \)
n = number of electrons

1 ampere is the current when 1 coulomb of charge flows through a conductor in 1 second. Small currents are measured in milliampere (1 mA = 10⁻³ A) and microampere (1 µA = 10⁻⁶ A).

  • By convention, the direction of current is the direction of flow of positive charge, which is opposite to the flow of electrons.
  • Current is measured with an ammeter, always connected in series. An ammeter has very low resistance.

Potential difference

Definition

The potential difference between two points in a circuit is the work done to move a unit charge from one point to the other.

Potential difference
\( V = \dfrac{W}{Q} \)
SI unit: volt (V) = J/C

1 volt is the potential difference between two points when 1 joule of work is done to move 1 coulomb of charge between them. Potential difference is measured with a voltmeter, always connected in parallel. A voltmeter has very high resistance. A cell or battery maintains the potential difference that makes charge flow.

Ohm’s law

Ohm’s law

The current through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided its temperature remains the same.

Ohm’s law
\( V = IR \)
R = resistance, SI unit ohm (Ω) = V/A

A graph of V against I for a metallic conductor is a straight line through the origin; its slope gives the resistance. 1 ohm is the resistance of a conductor when a potential difference of 1 V across it produces a current of 1 A.

Resistance is the property of a conductor to oppose the flow of charge through it. A rheostat is used to change the resistance, and hence the current, in a circuit.

Factors affecting resistance; resistivity

The resistance of a uniform conductor is directly proportional to its length \(l\), inversely proportional to its area of cross-section \(A\), and depends on the material and temperature.

Resistance of a wire
\( R = \rho\,\dfrac{l}{A} \)
\( \rho \) = resistivity, SI unit Ω m
  • Resistivity is a property of the material, not of the shape of the wire. It changes with temperature.
  • Metals and alloys have low resistivity (about 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁶ Ω m) and are good conductors. Insulators such as rubber and glass have very high resistivity (10¹² to 10¹⁷ Ω m).
  • Copper and aluminium are used for transmission wires because of their low resistivity.
  • Alloys such as nichrome have higher resistivity than pure metals and do not oxidise (burn) easily at high temperatures, so they are used in heaters, toasters and electric irons.
  • If a wire is stretched to double its length, its area halves, so its resistance becomes four times.

Why household appliances are connected in parallel: each appliance gets the full mains voltage (220 V), each can be switched on or off separately, a fault in one does not stop the others, and each draws only the current it needs.

Heating effect of electric current

When current flows through a resistor, part of the electrical energy is always converted into heat. This is the heating effect of electric current.

Joule’s law of heating
\( H = I^2 R t \)
H in joule; also \( H = VIt \)

Heat produced is directly proportional to (1) the square of the current, (2) the resistance, and (3) the time for which the current flows.

Applications

  • Electric iron, toaster, heater, kettle use heating elements made of nichrome.
  • Electric bulb: the filament is made of tungsten (melting point 3380 °C), which glows white-hot and gives light. Bulbs are filled with inert gases such as nitrogen and argon to make the filament last longer.
  • Electric fuse: a wire of low melting point (an alloy of metals such as aluminium, copper, iron or lead) connected in series. If the current becomes too large, the wire melts and breaks the circuit, protecting appliances. Common fuse ratings are 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A and 15 A.

Electric power

Definition

Electric power is the rate at which electrical energy is used or dissipated in a circuit.

Power
\( P = VI \)
SI unit: watt (W); 1 W = 1 V × 1 A
Other forms
\( P = I^2R = \dfrac{V^2}{R} \)
using V = IR
Commercial unit
\( 1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J} \)
1 kWh = 1 “unit” of electricity

Solved examples

A current of 0.5 A flows through a bulb for 10 minutes. How much charge flows through the circuit?

\( t = 10 \times 60 = 600\ \text{s} \)

\( Q = It = 0.5 \times 600 = 300\ \text{C} \)

Charge = 300 C.

How much work is done in moving a charge of 2 C across two points with a potential difference of 12 V?

\( W = VQ = 12 \times 2 = 24\ \text{J} \)

Work done = 24 J.

An electric heater draws 4 A from a 60 V supply. What current will it draw if the supply is increased to 120 V?

\( R = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{60}{4} = 15\ \Omega \)

At 120 V: \( I = \dfrac{120}{15} = 8\ \text{A} \)

Current = 8 A (doubling the voltage doubles the current).

Find the resistance of a copper wire 1 km long with an area of cross-section of 1 mm². (Resistivity of copper = \( 1.62 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\text{m} \))

\( l = 1000\ \text{m},\ A = 1\ \text{mm}^2 = 1 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2 \)

\( R = \rho\dfrac{l}{A} = \dfrac{1.62 \times 10^{-8} \times 1000}{1 \times 10^{-6}} = 16.2\ \Omega \)

Resistance = 16.2 Ω.

Resistors of 5 Ω, 10 Ω and 30 Ω are connected in parallel across a 12 V battery. Find the current through each resistor, the total current and the equivalent resistance.

\( I_1 = \dfrac{12}{5} = 2.4\ \text{A},\ I_2 = \dfrac{12}{10} = 1.2\ \text{A},\ I_3 = \dfrac{12}{30} = 0.4\ \text{A} \)

Total current \( I = 2.4 + 1.2 + 0.4 = 4\ \text{A} \)

\( \dfrac{1}{R_p} = \dfrac{1}{5} + \dfrac{1}{10} + \dfrac{1}{30} = \dfrac{6 + 3 + 1}{30} = \dfrac{1}{3} \Rightarrow R_p = 3\ \Omega \) (check: \( 12/3 = 4\ \text{A} \))

2.4 A, 1.2 A and 0.4 A; total 4 A; equivalent resistance 3 Ω.

The same three resistors (5 Ω, 10 Ω, 30 Ω) are now connected in series with the 12 V battery. Find the current and the potential difference across the 30 Ω resistor.

\( R_s = 5 + 10 + 30 = 45\ \Omega \); \( I = \dfrac{12}{45} = 0.27\ \text{A} \)

\( V_3 = IR_3 = \dfrac{12}{45} \times 30 = 8\ \text{V} \)

Current ≈ 0.27 A; potential difference across 30 Ω = 8 V.

An electric bulb is rated 100 W, 220 V. Find the current it draws and its resistance. How much energy does it use in 10 hours?

\( I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{100}{220} = 0.45\ \text{A} \)

\( R = \dfrac{V^2}{P} = \dfrac{220^2}{100} = 484\ \Omega \)

Energy \( = 0.1\ \text{kW} \times 10\ \text{h} = 1\ \text{kWh} \) (1 unit)

0.45 A; 484 Ω; 1 kWh.

A current of 4 A flows through a 20 Ω resistor for 30 s. How much heat is produced?

\( H = I^2Rt = 4^2 \times 20 \times 30 = 9600\ \text{J} \)

Heat produced = 9600 J = 9.6 kJ.

Quick revision

  • \( I = Q/t \) (ampere); \( V = W/Q \) (volt); \( e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \) C.
  • Ammeter in series (low resistance); voltmeter in parallel (high resistance).
  • Ohm’s law: \( V = IR \) at constant temperature; V–I graph is a straight line; unit of R is ohm (Ω).
  • \( R = \rho l/A \); resistivity depends on the material and temperature, not on the shape. Unit Ω m.
  • Series: \( R_s = R_1 + R_2 + \dots \), same current. Parallel: \( 1/R_p = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + \dots \), same voltage.
  • Homes use parallel wiring: every appliance gets 220 V and works independently.
  • Joule heating \( H = I^2Rt \): used in heaters (nichrome), bulbs (tungsten) and fuses (low melting point).
  • Power \( P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R \) (watt). 1 kWh = \( 3.6 \times 10^6 \) J.
