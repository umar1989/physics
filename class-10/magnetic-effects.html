Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

Magnetic field and field lines, field due to a straight wire, a circular loop and a solenoid, force on a current-carrying conductor, and domestic electric circuits.

Magnet and magnetic field

In 1820 Hans Christian Oersted noticed that a compass needle is deflected when a current flows through a nearby wire. This showed that electricity and magnetism are linked: a current-carrying conductor produces a magnetic field around it.

The region around a magnet in which its force can be detected is its magnetic field. It has both magnitude and direction. A compass needle is a small bar magnet; its north-pointing end is its north pole.

Magnetic field lines

Iron filings sprinkled around a bar magnet arrange themselves along curved lines called magnetic field lines.

Properties of magnetic field lines

1. Outside a magnet they go from the north pole to the south pole; inside the magnet they go from south to north. So they are closed curves.

2. The direction of the field at any point is given by the direction a compass needle points (the tangent to the field line).

3. Where the lines are crowded together, the field is stronger (as near the poles).

4. No two field lines ever cross, because at the crossing point a compass would have to point in two directions at once.

Field due to a straight current-carrying conductor

  • The field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire, in a plane perpendicular to it.
  • The field is stronger when the current is larger, and weaker farther from the wire.
  • Reversing the current reverses the direction of the field.
Right-hand thumb rule

Hold the current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand so that the thumb points in the direction of the current. Then your curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field lines. (This is also called Maxwell’s corkscrew rule.)

Field due to a circular loop

  • Near the wire, the field lines are nearly circles around it. As we move towards the centre, the circles become larger.
  • At the centre of the loop, the field lines are almost straight, and the field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop.
  • Every part of the loop adds to the field in the same direction at the centre. For a coil of \(n\) turns, the field is \(n\) times that of a single turn.
  • The field is stronger for a larger current and more turns, and weaker for a larger radius.

Field due to a solenoid

A solenoid is a long coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wound closely in the shape of a cylinder.

  • The field pattern around a solenoid is similar to that of a bar magnet: one end acts as a north pole and the other as a south pole.
  • Inside the solenoid, the field lines are parallel straight lines, so the field is uniform (the same at all points).

Electromagnet

If a soft iron core is placed inside a solenoid carrying current, it becomes a strong magnet called an electromagnet. It is a magnet only while current flows.

Force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field

André-Marie Ampère suggested that since a current exerts a force on a magnet, a magnet should exert an equal and opposite force on the current-carrying conductor. An aluminium rod carrying current and placed between the poles of a horseshoe magnet is indeed pushed sideways.

  • The force is largest when the current is perpendicular to the magnetic field.
  • Reversing either the current or the field reverses the direction of the force.
Fleming’s left-hand rule

Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand so that they are mutually perpendicular. If the forefinger points in the direction of the magnetic field and the middle finger in the direction of the current, then the thumb points in the direction of the force (motion) on the conductor.

This force is used in devices such as electric motors, loudspeakers and electric meters.

Domestic electric circuits

In India, electricity reaches homes as alternating current (AC) at 220 V and 50 Hz through the mains.

NoteNewer wiring in India uses the international colour code: live — brown, neutral — light blue, earth — green with yellow stripes. For exams, follow the NCERT colours above.

  • The wires pass through the electricity meter and a main fuse (or MCB) before reaching the distribution box.
  • Houses usually have two circuits: a 15 A circuit for high-power appliances (geysers, air coolers, electric irons) and a 5 A circuit for fans and bulbs.
  • All appliances are connected in parallel, each with its own switch, so each gets 220 V and works independently.

Earthing

The metal body of appliances such as electric irons, toasters, refrigerators and washing machines is connected to the earth wire. If the live wire accidentally touches the metal body, the current flows safely into the ground through this low-resistance path, and the user does not get a severe electric shock.

Short circuit and overloading

  • Short circuit: the live and neutral wires come into direct contact (for example, when insulation is damaged). The resistance becomes very small and the current becomes very large.
  • Overloading: too many appliances are connected to one socket, or the supply voltage rises suddenly, and the total current exceeds the safe limit.
  • Both can overheat wires and cause fires. The fuse (or a miniature circuit breaker, MCB) protects the circuit by breaking it when the current exceeds the safe value.

Solved examples

A current flows vertically upwards through a straight wire. Looking down on it from above, in which direction do the magnetic field lines go around the wire?

Point the right thumb upwards (direction of current). The fingers curl anticlockwise when seen from above.

The field lines are anticlockwise circles when viewed from above.

A horizontal wire carries current from west to east in a magnetic field that points vertically downwards. In which direction is the force on the wire?

Using Fleming’s left-hand rule: forefinger (field) downwards, middle finger (current) towards east.

The thumb then points towards the north.

The force on the wire is towards the north.

An electric oven rated 2 kW is used in a domestic circuit (220 V) that has a current rating of 5 A. What will happen?

\( I = \dfrac{P}{V} = \dfrac{2000}{220} = 9.09\ \text{A} \)

This is more than the 5 A rating of the circuit.

The circuit is overloaded; the fuse will melt (or the MCB will trip) and cut off the supply.

A house has a 1 kW electric iron, a 1.5 kW geyser and four 60 W bulbs all switched on together on a 220 V supply. Find the total current. Is a 10 A fuse enough?

Total power \( = 1000 + 1500 + 4 \times 60 = 2740\ \text{W} \)

\( I = \dfrac{2740}{220} = 12.5\ \text{A} \)

Total current ≈ 12.5 A, so a 10 A fuse would blow; a 15 A circuit is needed.

Quick revision

  • A current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field (Oersted, 1820).
  • Field lines: N → S outside the magnet, closed curves, never cross; crowded lines mean a stronger field.
  • Straight wire: concentric circles; direction by the right-hand thumb rule.
  • Circular loop: field nearly straight at the centre; increases with the number of turns and current.
  • Solenoid: behaves like a bar magnet; uniform field inside; with a soft iron core it becomes an electromagnet.
  • Force on a conductor is maximum when current ⟂ field; direction by Fleming’s left-hand rule (Forefinger–Field, Middle–Current, Thumb–Force).
  • Domestic supply: 220 V AC, 50 Hz. Live (red), neutral (black), earth (green). Appliances in parallel.
  • Earthing protects against shocks. Fuse/MCB protects against short circuit and overloading.
